Overview

What happens when product teams can't hear their users?

Product teams at large digital companies receive tens of thousands of app store reviews every month. Reading them is impossible. Acting on them is even harder. Most teams settle for star ratings and keyword counts — a signal so blunt it barely qualifies as insight.

UserHear is Commencis's answer: an AI-powered analytics platform that ingests reviews from the App Store, Google Play, and social channels, then surfaces structured, prioritised, actionable intelligence — with the emotional texture of the original feedback intact.

As Senior Lead UX Designer, I own the end-to-end product vision and roadmap, shaping both the strategic direction and the moment-to-moment design execution with my team.

The Problem

Reviews exist. Understanding them doesn't.

Before UserHear, client teams were doing one of three things: ignoring reviews entirely, manually reading samples, or exporting CSVs into spreadsheets and hoping someone would draw conclusions. None of these worked at scale.

Before Status quo

  • Manual review sampling — 2–3 hours per sprint
  • Star rating used as proxy for user sentiment
  • No connection between releases and feedback shifts
  • No feature-level visibility into what users talk about
  • PMs rely on intuition, not data, to prioritise backlog

After UserHear

  • Automated ingestion from App Store, Play, social
  • Feature-level sentiment tracked across time
  • Version impact: see what each release did to mood
  • AI-generated recommendations fed into roadmap
  • Full team alignment on what users actually experience

"I don't want to read 4,000 reviews. I want to know what to fix next sprint — and why."

— Product manager, discovery interview

Three core tensions shaped every design decision: signal vs. noise, confidence vs. humility (how do you show AI certainty without misleading?), and action vs. exploration.

Problem

Too many reviews, scattered across call centres, app stores, surveys and social — with almost none of it acted on.

Solution

A four-stepped intelligence loop — Listen, Understand, Deep dive, Uncover — turns raw feedback into a roadmap.

Solution

Sector-agnostic and built on a Turkish-first sentiment model, with every insight traceable back to its source.

Solution

Qualitative feedback, finally actionable — prioritised recommendations product teams can ship against.

Product Strategy

Listen, understand, act.

The product strategy is organised around a three-stage intelligence loop. Each stage has a distinct design character — its own information density, interaction model, and visual language.

01 · Listen

Collect

Ingest reviews from App Store, Google Play, and social channels — all in one place, refreshed automatically.

02 · Understand

Analyse

Feature attribution, multilingual sentiment, emotion detection, intent analysis — powered by top LLMs, optimised for Turkish.

03 · Deep Dive

Correlate

Track how each release affected sentiment. Pinpoint which product decisions drove changes.

04 · Uncover

Recommend

AI-generated improvement recommendations with reasoning and evidence — ready for the roadmap.

05 · AI Layer

Explore

Ask "Why did sentiment drop last month?" in natural language and get a structured, evidence-backed answer instantly.

Key UX decisions

1

Insight feed as home screen. The default view is not a chart dashboard — it's a prioritised feed of AI-generated insights, each anchored to a confidence level and drill-down to source reviews.

2

Confidence is shown, never hidden. Saying "based on 342 reviews, confidence: high" is actionable. Hiding uncertainty feels polished but erodes trust over time.

3

Progressive disclosure for depth. Every metric starts as a number → expands to a chart → drills into raw reviews — without ever losing context of where you started.

4

Turkish-language-first sentiment model. We evaluated and selected models specifically optimised for Turkish morphology and informality — a capability no global competitor offers out of the box.

Design Decisions

The hardest part of AI UX: building trust.

Designing for AI-generated output means doing something unusual: making the output feel authoritative enough to act on, and humble enough to question.

01

Evidence chains over black boxes

Every insight links to the specific reviews that generated it. Users can always ask "why?" and get a real answer — a decision that slows reading slightly but dramatically increases trust.

02

Quantified confidence, not qualitative hedging

We always show the n. "Based on 342 reviews, confidence: high" is actionable; "high confidence AI analysis" is not.

03

Sentiment as emotion, not just polarity

UserHear surfaces emotional texture — frustration, surprise, delight, confusion — because teams need to understand how users feel, not just which direction they feel it.

04

AI chat as the power user's interface

The chat interface is a secondary mode, not the primary one. Research showed most users want structured views first — the chat accelerates experts who know what they're looking for.

05

Shareable outputs for non-users

The most impactful insight often reaches someone who doesn't log in. Report and deck generation is core to the product — so insights can spread to stakeholders who will never open the app.

"The best AI interface is one where users feel smarter, not replaced."

— Design principle, UserHear

Reflection

What I'm still learning.

The hardest challenge has been calibrating user expectations — not the AI model itself. Early users either over-trusted the output (treating it as ground truth) or under-trusted it (verifying every insight before acting). The right level of epistemic humility in the interface is an ongoing experiment.

I've also learned that AI product design is unusually dependent on prompt quality and model selection. A designer who understands these layers — and can advocate for design decisions that affect them — is a different kind of designer than one who only works at the UI layer.

Key takeaway

"The interface is the last mile of an AI system. But it's also the only mile users experience. Getting it right means understanding everything that comes before it."

Overview

İşCep is the mobile banking application of İşbank — Turkey's largest private bank, with millions of daily active users. The Challenge

Banking design is never just design.

Mobile banking applications carry extraordinary user weight. Every interaction involves trust, money, and — for a significant portion of users — genuine anxiety. The bar for quality isn't just aesthetic: a confusing form field can mean a missed payment; an ambiguous error message can mean a panic call to customer support.

At the same time, İşCep was under pressure to modernise rapidly — matching the pace of fintech challengers while maintaining the reliability expectations of a 100-year-old institution. These are genuinely opposing forces.

↕

Speed vs. safety

Fintech apps ship weekly. İşbank has compliance, legal reviews, and a user base that includes elderly first-time smartphone users. Every decision had to reconcile both realities.

⬡

Scale vs. craft

13 designers working in parallel on different features creates drift. Maintaining visual and experiential coherence required systemic thinking, not just design oversight.

⇄

Client vs. agency

Commencis is an agency. İşbank is the client. Aligning across two organisations — with different risk tolerances and timelines — is a leadership challenge as much as a design one.

∞

Legacy vs. future

İşCep has millions of users with established mental models. Every IA change had to account for who might break, and how many — a constraint that makes every decision weightier.

Leading the Team

Building a team that thinks, not just executes.

Managing 13 designers across research and design streams is fundamentally a communication and culture challenge. My approach rested on three pillars: shared language, visible quality standards, and genuine creative ownership for every designer.

Shared language

Weekly design principles sessions — not work reviews, but discussions. "What does 'trustworthy' mean in a mobile UI?" produced better decisions than pixel-pushing feedback.

Visible standards

Critique cadences with documented rubrics. Not "I don't like this" but "this fails the contrast accessibility check." Specificity enables accountability without creating fear.

Creative ownership

Designers were assigned feature areas, not tasks. They owned features from research through handoff — building product intuition, not just execution speed.

Roadmap contribution

Design had a seat at the roadmap table. I supported roadmap creation through design-led data — UX metrics, usability findings, competitive analysis — not just aesthetic preference.

"The best design managers protect two things: the team's time and the team's craft. Everything else is negotiable."

— Leadership principle, İşCep design team

Design Process

From insight to shipped screen.

The process was adapted from double-diamond, pressure-tested for 13 designers working in parallel across multiple feature squads. The critical innovation was a "design checkpoint" system that kept quality consistent without creating bottlenecks.

01

Discovery

Research sprints with the bank's CX team

User interviews, session recordings, and app store review analysis (powered by UserHear) formed the evidence base for every design initiative.

02

Definition

Problem framing with PM and engineering

A shared problem statement, success criteria, and scope boundary — agreed by design, product, engineering, and client — before any design work begins. Single biggest time-saver.

03

Ideation

Parallel concepting with structured critique

Multiple directions — not to increase volume, but to surface trade-offs early. "I like / I wish / what if" framework keeps feedback generative, not evaluative.

04

Prototyping

High-fidelity in Figma, tested with real users

Prototypes tested with actual İşbank customers recruited through the bank's user panel. Think-aloud protocol. Specific, actionable findings.

05

Delivery

Design checkpoint → engineering handoff

Mandatory gate: lead review, accessibility audit, component library compliance check before any handoff. Eliminated ~80% of revision loops.

Selected Work

A few of the initiatives I led.

İşCep work is under NDA. What follows describes the design challenges and decisions behind selected initiatives — not specific screens.

→

Information architecture redesign

The existing bottom navigation had 7 tabs accumulated over years of feature additions. Card sorting with 40 users, three competing IA proposals, all three tested in prototype. The winner reduced time-to-task on the five most common banking operations measurably.

→

Money transfer flow simplification

The transfer flow — the app's most-used feature — had 6 screens. Analysis showed users abandoning at confirmation, nervously re-checking details. Redesigned around progressive disclosure: all details upfront, confirmation as a summary not a form. Abandonment at the confirmation step dropped.

→

Accessibility uplift across core flows

Systematic audit found 38% of colour combinations failing WCAG AA contrast. Token-level audit of the design system, updated every out-of-compliance value, and built automated contrast checking into the Figma process going forward.

→

Modular onboarding framework

Every new product launch (savings, insurance, investment) previously required onboarding designed from scratch. I created a modular onboarding system — reusable across product types — reducing design time for new product launches by ~60%.

DesignOps

The systems that make design work.

With 13 designers working simultaneously, the biggest quality risk isn't individual skill — it's drift. Components used inconsistently. Accessibility standards applied selectively. Naming conventions that differ between teams. These small inconsistencies compound into a product that feels like it was designed by many different people, because it was.

I invested significantly in DesignOps as a force multiplier: building the systems, standards, and rituals that let the team maintain quality without requiring my review on every screen.

DesignOps interventions

1

Component library governance. Naming convention, contribution process, and deprecation policy for the Figma component library. Any designer can add a component; any addition goes through a review gate. The library grew without becoming chaotic.

2

Weekly design critique cadence. Rotating presenters. Structured rubric ("does this meet the accessibility standard?" not "do I like this?"). Reviews became a learning environment, not a judgment environment.

3

12-point handoff checklist. Mandatory before any design goes to engineering: contrast ratios, touch targets, error/loading/empty states, responsive breakpoints, copy review. Eliminated the most common sources of revision requests from engineering.

4

Research templates. Standardised interview guides, affinity mapping templates, and synthesis formats. Any designer could run a research sprint without reinventing the process. Reduced research setup time by ~50%.

"Good DesignOps is invisible — designers shouldn't notice the system, they should just find it easier to do great work."

— Design principle, Commencis

Outcomes

What 1.5 years looks like.

The outcomes are multidimensional — product quality, team capability, and organisational practice all improved in measurable ways. The most meaningful outcome is harder to quantify: a design team that got better at design over the course of the engagement, not just faster.

01

App store UX sentiment improved

Monthly UX-specific app store sentiment (tracked via UserHear) showed consistent improvement across the core banking flows redesigned by the team.

02

Design velocity increased

With DesignOps systems in place, the team shipped more features per sprint without a proportional increase in revision requests — quality, not just speed.

03

Team capability visibly grew

Designers who joined as mid-level practitioners were doing lead-level work within 6–8 months. The critique culture and ownership model accelerated growth.

04

Design sat at strategy

By the end, design was contributing to roadmap decisions with data — not just being handed features to design. This shift took 12 deliberate months.

Reflection

What leading at scale actually taught me.

I came into this role thinking design leadership was primarily about design decisions. I left understanding it's primarily about communication — between disciplines, between organisations, between levels of abstraction. The best design leader isn't the best designer in the room; it's the person who makes everyone in the room better at design.

The hardest moment was realising that protecting the team's creative energy sometimes meant absorbing stakeholder pressure myself — not passing it downstream. That's not heroism; it's what the role requires. And it's what builds trust with a team faster than anything else.

Key takeaway

"Design quality at scale isn't a design problem. It's a culture problem that systems, rituals, and leadership can solve — but only if you treat it as seriously as any product problem."
