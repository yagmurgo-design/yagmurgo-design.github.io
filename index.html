Overview

What happens when product teams can't hear their users?

Product teams at large digital companies receive tens of thousands of app store reviews every month. Reading them is impossible. Acting on them is even harder. Most teams settle for star ratings and keyword counts — a signal so blunt it barely qualifies as insight.

UserHear is Commencis's answer: an AI-powered analytics platform that ingests reviews from the App Store, Google Play, and social channels, then surfaces structured, prioritised, actionable intelligence — with the emotional texture of the original feedback intact.

As Senior Lead UX Designer, I own the end-to-end product vision and roadmap, shaping both the strategic direction and the moment-to-moment design execution with my team.

The Problem

Reviews exist. Understanding them doesn't.

Before UserHear, client teams were doing one of three things: ignoring reviews entirely, manually reading samples, or exporting CSVs into spreadsheets and hoping someone would draw conclusions. None of these worked at scale.

Before Status quo

  • Manual review sampling — 2–3 hours per sprint
  • Star rating used as proxy for user sentiment
  • No connection between releases and feedback shifts
  • No feature-level visibility into what users talk about
  • PMs rely on intuition, not data, to prioritise backlog

After UserHear

  • Automated ingestion from App Store, Play, social
  • Feature-level sentiment tracked across time
  • Version impact: see what each release did to mood
  • AI-generated recommendations fed into roadmap
  • Full team alignment on what users actually experience

"I don't want to read 4,000 reviews. I want to know what to fix next sprint — and why."

— Product manager, discovery interview

Three core tensions shaped every design decision: signal vs. noise, confidence vs. humility (how do you show AI certainty without misleading?), and action vs. exploration.

Problem

Too many reviews, scattered across call centres, app stores, surveys and social — with almost none of it acted on.

Solution

A four-stepped intelligence loop — Listen, Understand, Deep dive, Uncover — turns raw feedback into a roadmap.

Solution

Sector-agnostic and built on a Turkish-first sentiment model, with every insight traceable back to its source.

Listen, understand, act.

Key UX decisions

1

Insight feed as home screen. The default view is not a chart dashboard — it's a prioritised feed of AI-generated insights, each anchored to a confidence level and drill-down to source reviews.

2

Confidence is shown, never hidden. Saying "based on 342 reviews, confidence: high" is actionable. Hiding uncertainty feels polished but erodes trust over time.

3

Progressive disclosure for depth. Every metric starts as a number → expands to a chart → drills into raw reviews — without ever losing context of where you started.

4

Turkish-language-first sentiment model. We evaluated and selected models specifically optimised for Turkish morphology and informality — a capability no global competitor offers out of the box.

Design Decisions

The hardest part of AI UX: building trust.

Designing for AI-generated output means doing something unusual: making the output feel authoritative enough to act on, and humble enough to question.

01

Evidence chains over black boxes

Every insight links to the specific reviews that generated it. Users can always ask "why?" and get a real answer — a decision that slows reading slightly but dramatically increases trust.

02

Quantified confidence, not qualitative hedging

We always show the n. "Based on 342 reviews, confidence: high" is actionable; "high confidence AI analysis" is not.

03

Sentiment as emotion, not just polarity

UserHear surfaces emotional texture — frustration, surprise, delight, confusion — because teams need to understand how users feel, not just which direction they feel it.

04

AI chat as the power user's interface

The chat interface is a secondary mode, not the primary one. Research showed most users want structured views first — the chat accelerates experts who know what they're looking for.

05

Shareable outputs for non-users

The most impactful insight often reaches someone who doesn't log in. Report and deck generation is core to the product — so insights can spread to stakeholders who will never open the app.

"The best AI interface is one where users feel smarter, not replaced."

— Design principle, UserHear

Reflection

What I'm still learning.

The hardest challenge has been calibrating user expectations — not the AI model itself. Early users either over-trusted the output (treating it as ground truth) or under-trusted it (verifying every insight before acting). The right level of epistemic humility in the interface is an ongoing experiment.

I've also learned that AI product design is unusually dependent on prompt quality and model selection. A designer who understands these layers — and can advocate for design decisions that affect them — is a different kind of designer than one who only works at the UI layer.

Key takeaway

"The interface is the last mile of an AI system. But it's also the only mile users experience. Getting it right means understanding everything that comes before it."

Overview

One app, three ways of shaping it.

İşCep is the mobile banking app of İşbank — Turkey's largest private bank. It serves millions of customers every day through hundreds of functions, from a first transfer to a first trade. I've spent five years designing it alongside the bank's product teams, and the work has settled into three tracks that feed each other.

Some of it is products: new experiences built with individual product teams. Some of it is process: the design and product development cycles the bank uses to build anything for İşCep. And once a year it is vision: research and workshops that decide what the app should become next, and seed the following year's roadmap.

Vision

Where the roadmap comes from.

Every year the bank and Commencis run a vision cycle: squads research for weeks, then a workshop turns the findings into ideas the bank can score, detail and place on a roadmap. What I've watched change is the output — from post-its on a wall to working demos on a phone.

Leading the team

Building a team that thinks, not just executes.

Alongside the work itself, I led a team of 13 designers across research and design. Managing that many people in parallel is fundamentally a communication and culture challenge, so my approach rested on three pillars: shared language, visible quality standards, and genuine creative ownership for every designer.

Shared language

Weekly design principles sessions — not work reviews, but discussions. "What does 'trustworthy' mean in a mobile UI?" produced better decisions than pixel-pushing feedback.

Visible standards

Critique cadences with documented rubrics. Not "I don't like this" but "this fails the contrast accessibility check." Specificity enables accountability without creating fear.

Creative ownership

Designers were assigned feature areas, not tasks. They owned features from research through handoff — building product intuition, not just execution speed.

Roadmap contribution

Design had a seat at the roadmap table, supported by design-led data — UX metrics, usability findings, competitive analysis — not aesthetic preference. The vision cycle is where that seat is earned every year.

Key takeaway

"Design quality at scale isn't a design problem. It's a culture problem that systems, rituals and leadership can solve — but only if you treat it as seriously as any product problem."

Overview

A redesign with millions of habits inside it.

İşCep 10.0 — presented to the bank's Digital Banking department in July 2024 — set the direction for a new İşCep: a smart, personal, innovative finance and life app. It wasn't a reskin. It was a plan to change how the app is organised, how it addresses different customers, and how quickly it can evolve, while keeping it familiar to everyone who opens it every day.

That last part is the real design problem. A redesign at this scale is less about screens than about change: every decision in the information architecture had to account for who might get lost, and how many of them there would be.

Vision

One sentence, three pillars.

The 10.0 vision fits in a line — Smart, Personal, Innovative Finance and Life App — and rests on three focus areas that every workstream had to trace back to.

01

Personal-data-driven experience

An app that reorganises itself around how each customer actually uses it.

02

Digital sales, context-driven

Offers and next steps that appear at the moment they make sense.

03

Smart and all-in-one

One place for banking and the life around it.

The first pillar drove most of the redesign. Its five sub-themes became the backbone of the design brief:

01

Product areas shaped by usage habits

Separate spaces for retail, small-business and commercial customers instead of one menu for everyone.

02

A simpler information architecture

Fewer competing entry points, and fewer component variants doing the same job.

03

A personalisable home

The first screen adapts to the customer instead of showing everyone the same thing.

04

A way back to the old look

An explicit escape hatch for customers who aren't ready to change.

05

Products and transactions offered at the moment they're relevant.

Interaction vision

Turning a crowded closet into a custom-fitted wardrobe.

The interaction vision is our answer to one question: how should the redesign make users feel? Today İşCep can feel like a closet where everything is somewhere, if you dig for it. The redesign should feel like a wardrobe built around its owner: what you use most within reach, the rest neatly in its place.

Approach

Design the change, not only the screens.

Key decisions

1

Personalise the surface, not the structure. Every customer sees a different home, but the underlying navigation stays predictable, so a customer's mental model survives the change.

2

Ship an escape hatch. The customers who most need a way back are also the ones most likely to churn if they feel forced forward.

3

Design for the component reality. Three generations of components coexist in the app. The redesign had to work with all of them while migrating toward one.

4

Test before build. Concepts passed the design cycle's pre-development gate: usability testing with customers, scaled to the size of the change, before a line of code.

Overview

Banking, the way people actually plan their lives.

İşCep's functions are organised the way a bank is organised: by product. People don't think that way. They think in moments — buying a car, moving house, starting a family, planning a trip — and each of those touches loans, insurance, payments and offers scattered across the app.

İşCep'le Hayatım — "life with İşCep" — gives each of those moments its own space: Aracım (my car), Evim (my home), Ailem (my family) and Seyahatim (my travel). It was the first phase of the new İşCep roadmap.

Insight

A product-shaped app, life-shaped needs.

Discovery started where every request in the cycle starts: a qualitative-plus-quantitative baseline. Usage funnels, the bank's customer-issue records, app-store and forum feedback, and a competitor benchmark told us where people got lost between the need and the product that met it.

Before Product-shaped

  • A car-related need means visiting loans, insurance and payments separately
  • Offers live in a campaigns area, away from the need
  • Nothing tells customers what's relevant to their situation
  • Cross-product journeys depend on knowing product names

After Life-shaped

  • One space per life domain, the customer's own products first
  • Related services and offers shown in context
  • The app reflects what the customer already has
  • New domains can be added without redesigning navigation

Concept

Four spaces, one pattern.

Each space follows the same structure — what you have, what's coming up, what you could do next — so learning one teaches the other three.

🚗

Aracım · My car

Vehicle financing, insurance, tolls and fines, and offers around running a car.

🏠

Evim · My home

Home loans, home insurance, utility bills and everything that comes with a place of your own.

👨‍👩‍👧

Ailem · My family

Shared financial life: children's savings, protection and the payments a household repeats.

✈️

Seyahatim · My travel

Currency, travel cover, spending abroad and offers before, during and after a trip.

The same life-domain logic came back later in the annual vision workshops, where travel, family and child journeys were proposed as ideas in their own right.

Decisions

What made it feel right.

1

Start from what the customer already has. A space that opens with a sales pitch feels like an ad. One that opens with the customer's own car loan feels like a tool.

2

Suggest in context, never as a banner. An offer belongs next to the need it serves, at the moment it makes sense.

3

One pattern, four spaces. Shared structure and components mean a fifth space costs a content brief, not a redesign.

4

Design the empty state. A customer without a car still opens Aracım. The cycle requires error, partial and empty states, not only the happy path — and here the empty state is the front door.

Overview

Trading, in an app built for banking.

Mobil Borsa brings stock-market trading into İşCep, designed with the bank's capital markets team. The brief sounds simple — let customers see the market, place an order and follow their portfolio — but it puts a high-stakes, high-frequency activity inside an app designed for everyday banking, and asks it to stand next to dedicated investing apps.

The investor

Who trades, and who they listen to.

Research from the annual vision cycle split the bank's customers into four groups. Two of them matter most here.

Investor

Saving and investing habits are established, and they follow many sources. The goal is to earn more. They take advice from Telegram, YouTube and friends rather than the bank — bank suggestions have historically returned too little — and they're sensitive to commissions and fees. Open to the bank in principle, not in practice.

Everyday user

Invests at low-to-medium risk but watches less closely. Motivated by staying balanced and moving slowly upward, and afraid of a large loss. Uses many banking products, is open to new ones, and open to suggestions.

The opportunity: transparent, competitive tools for the first group — and low-threshold starting points for the second.

— Vision research, engagement opportunity

Challenges

Four tensions in one screen.

↕

Speed vs. safety

An order is money moving fast. The form has to be quick for experts and forgiving for beginners.

◐

Transparency vs. simplicity

Investors are fee-sensitive, so commission has to be visible before confirmation without cluttering the flow.

§

Information vs. advice

In regulated investing, "recommendation" carries legal weight. The interface has to inform without advising.

⇄

One interface, several investors

First-timers and active traders need different depth. One screen for both risks serving neither.

Concept

Market first, depth on demand.

The concept treats trading as three moments — watching, acting and reviewing — and keeps each one to a single job.

Watch

A watchlist and market overview that answer "what's happening?" in a glance.

⚡

Act

Order entry with costs and consequences visible before the final tap.

📈

Review

A portfolio and performance view that answers "how am I doing?" honestly.

🌱

Start

A gentle first-trade path for customers who have never invested.

Decisions

Where the details earned their keep.

1

Show the cost before the tap. Commission and fees appear on the order screen, not after it. For a fee-sensitive customer, transparency is the product.

2

Simple by default, deep on demand. The same flow serves first-timers and active traders by revealing depth progressively, rather than splitting the app in two.

3

Status is never ambiguous. An order is always pending, partially filled, filled or cancelled — and the interface says which. Users should never wonder whether it worked.

4

Words are a design material. Copy was reviewed with the business unit so the app presents information and options, not advice.

A process is a product with users too.

İşCep isn't built by one team. Many product teams, the bank's channel and experience teams, agencies and technology subsidiaries all ship into the same app. Without a shared path from request to release, every team invents its own — and the customer feels the seams.

2024 · The diagnosis

Everyone who builds for İşCep, in the same room.

İşCep 10.0 exposed a gap. The design cycle described how design worked, but the teams building for İşCep had very different levels of familiarity with digital development and with the İşCep channel. So we asked them.

Six 90-minute brainstorm sessions brought in the bank's digital banking, channel and experience teams alongside product teams from payments, capital markets, treasury, deposits, insurance and commercial products — product owners, managers, analysts and MCM developers. In each, participants walked us through four stages, first as they are today and then as they should be.

Reflection

What building a process taught me.

The most useful part of the diagnosis wasn't any single finding; it was that teams who rarely met saw the same problems from different sides. A shared map doesn't just speed teams up — it gives them a way to disagree productively.

The other lesson is humility. The follow-up-and-improvement stage was the least mature of the four, with few artefacts behind it. A process, like a product, ships unfinished and improves against the people who use it.

Key takeaway

"A good process is invisible to the customer and obvious to the team. If people have to ask what happens next, the process isn't finished."

Overview

Where the roadmap comes from.

Every year, İşbank's digital banking team and Commencis run a vision cycle to decide where İşCep goes next. It starts with a focus — a set of questions the bank wants to explore in depth. Past focuses have included hyper-personalisation, İşCep for everyone, Maximum card brand identity communication through İşCep and personal data orientedness for all; the constant thread is making the app as personal as possible and using capabilities of AI to transform digital banking.

We treat every vision process as a small-scale design project. From the very beginning, we take an end-to-end approach; building a deep understanding of the product’s current state, its users, and relevant industry and technology trends. These insights help us uncover opportunity areas and identify the key challenges to address. The output isn't a deck that gets filed. Ideas that come out of the cycle feed the next year's product roadmap, which makes it the front door of annual planning — and, once the ideas become projects, the front door of the product development cycle too.

The workshop day

Designed for leaving with something.

The workshop's manifesto is one line: be goal- and output-focused — we want to leave with something in our hands. That's why there's a physical aspect to it: printed cards, use of wall space, stickers, and a designer facilitating every group to explain how the user and business impact will be achieved through the idea presented.

Anatomy of an idea card

1

Name and concept. One idea per card, with a sketch and competitor examples so it reads without a presenter.

2

Value. What the business gains and what the customer gains, with evidence from customer comments.

3

AARRR. The idea mapped to the pirate funnel, so it's tied to a metric before it's tied to a screen.

4

Goals. Success metrics detailed enough to be checked later.

01

45–90 mins

A gallery walk of the ideas through idea cards and/or working prototypes. Participants take notes and/or think aloud while the responsible designer actively listens and facilitates a fruitful conversation.

45–60 mins

Evaluate and prioritise

Based on the review, participants score each idea, considering business and user impact as well as its technical requirements. Once everyone has scored, the winning ideas, low-hanging fruits and ideas that need reconsidering become clear.

Reflection

What the cycle keeps teaching.

Five things I take into every cycle.

1

Team facilitation. Everyone has to run the same plan with the same shared understanding. That makes staying in touch with the responsible designer in each squad, reviewing their outputs and giving guidance when needed the most important part of the job.

2

Keep stakeholders in the loop. Share interim outputs as decks or reports and, where possible, hold 45-minute sessions to walk through them with room for Q&A. It's what makes stakeholders own the process.

3

Vision day: as tangible and as thorough as possible. Participants should relate directly to the ideas, so we use every artefact that helps. Business and user impact have to be made concrete and talked through, not just listed.

4

The designer as catalyst. After working this closely with the data and getting to know the product, I can also support the bank in turning ideas into projects and planning them. That speeds the whole process up.

5

In 2027, AI made us about 3× faster. We created skills and markdown files for the repeatable steps of the vision cycle. Every squad had its own project structure, all continuously fed by an umbrella project. The whole team experienced vibe coding, and in two months we vibe-coded all of İşCep's individual, commercial and investor flows.
